Wall and Drywall Water Damage in Winchester

A sheet of drywall behaves much as a wick does. Once the base of a wall gets wet, moisture climbs through the paper and gypsum, commonly a foot or more higher than the water stood on the floor. The painted face can look and feel normal while the back side and the insulation behind it are soaked. Looks cannot be trusted here, so every wall gets tested with a meter.

A wet wall does not automatically mean demolition. Drywall that got wet with clean water and is still firm can often be dried in place, sometimes by removing the baseboard and moving air through small holes into the cavity. If the drywall has gone soft, crumbles, shows mold or took in dirty water, we do a flood cut, meaning a level cut over the damaged area with all material beneath it removed.

What It Looks Like

  • Baseboards that swell, warp or pull away from the wall
  • Dark spots or fuzzy growth at corners and behind furniture
  • Wet tack strip, lifting planks or stained flooring at the base of the wall
  • Mildew smell you notice most when standing close to one section of wall
  • Cool, slightly clammy drywall next to walls at normal temperature
  • Drywall that feels soft, spongy or crumbles when pressed
What You Cannot See

What Hides Behind It

Insulation Holding Water

Fiberglass batts soak up water and hold it against the studs. No amount of airflow on the painted side will dry a cavity packed with wet batts.

Growth Behind the Paint

The back of the drywall is dark, still and lined with paper, which is ideal for mold. It can cover the hidden side while the painted face looks clean.

Wet Sheathing Outside

Outside walls have sheathing past the insulation that can also get soaked. That layer dries slowly and is checked before the wall is closed.

Wet Outlets and Wiring

Receptacles and cables sit low in the wall, right where water collects. Do not use outlets on a wet wall until the circuit has been checked.

Our Process

How We Handle Wall and Drywall Water Damage

1

Find Every Wet Area

Every wall is checked with a thermal camera and moisture meter, and we mark the height and reach of the water, covering the other face and the rooms next door.

2

Decide What Stays

Based on the water category, how long it sat and how the drywall feels, we tell you plainly which sections can be dried in place and which need to come out.

3

Cut or Ventilate

Walls we are keeping have their baseboards removed so air can be pushed into the cavity. On sections coming out, a straight flood cut is made and soaked insulation is removed.

4

Drying and Reconstruction

Air movers and dehumidifiers run until studs and remaining drywall read dry, typically 3 to 5 days. After that comes insulation, new drywall hung and finished, trim put back and paint.

If Nothing Is Done

What Happens if You Wait

First hours

Water Starts Climbing

The bottom edge of the drywall takes in water from the floor and it begins moving up. The trim and the framing plate at floor level get wet too.

Hours 24 to 48

Swelling and Blisters

The wet line rises higher, baseboards swell and paint begins to blister. Growth is able to get going on the paper that lines the hidden side.

Days 3 through 7

Drywall Breaks Down

The gypsum core turns soft and its paper starts to separate. Insulation slumps inside the wall, odor builds and mold spreads across the hidden back side.

Past one week

Framing Is Affected

Studs and plates stay wet long enough to grow mold and begin to decay. Much more wall now has to be removed and rebuilt.
